1.1 What is Additive Manufacturing (AM)?

1.1.1 Definition of Additive Manufacturing

Additive manufacturing (AM) is defined by the ISO/ASTM 52900:2021 standard as "a process of joining materials to make objects from 3D model data, usually layer upon layer". In contrast to conventional subtractive machining (material removal), AM adds material only where it is needed, giving it the following innovative characteristics:

  • Design freedom: Enables manufacturing of complex geometries impossible with conventional methods (hollow structures, lattice structures, topology-optimized shapes)
  • Material efficiency: Since material is used only where needed, material waste is 5-10% (versus 30-90% waste with conventional machining)
  • On-demand manufacturing: Enables low-volume, high-mix production of customized products without tooling
  • Part consolidation: Structures that previously required assembling multiple components can be built as a single piece, reducing assembly steps
💡 Industrial Significance

The AM market is growing rapidly. According to the Wohlers Report 2023:

  • Global AM market size: $18.3B (2023) → projected $83.9B (2030, 23.5% CAGR)
  • Application breakdown: prototyping (38%), tooling (27%), end-use parts (35%)
  • Key industries: aerospace (26%), medical (21%), automotive (18%), consumer goods (15%)
  • Material share: polymers (55%), metals (35%), ceramics (7%), other (3%)

1.1.3 Key Application Areas of AM

Application 1: Rapid Prototyping

The first major use of AM, producing prototypes rapidly for design verification, functional testing, and market evaluation:

  • Shortened lead time: Conventional prototyping (weeks to months) → AM reduces this to hours or days
  • Accelerated design iteration: Multiple versions can be prototyped at low cost to optimize the design
  • Improved communication: Visual and tactile physical models help align stakeholders' understanding
  • Typical examples: Automotive design models, appliance housing prototypes, pre-surgical simulation models for medical devices

Application 2: Tooling & Fixtures

Using AM to manufacture jigs, tools, and molds used on the shop floor:

  • Custom jigs: Rapid production of assembly and inspection jigs tailored to specific production lines
  • Conformal cooling molds: Injection molds with 3D cooling channels that follow the product shape, rather than conventional straight channels (reducing cooling time by 30-70%)
  • Lightweight tooling: Lightweight end-effectors using lattice structures to reduce operator burden
  • Typical examples: BMW assembly line jigs (over 100,000 units manufactured annually via AM), TaylorMade golf driver molds

Application 3: End-Use Parts

Directly manufacturing final products with AM has surged in recent years:

  • Aerospace components: GE Aviation LEAP fuel injection nozzles (previously 20 parts, consolidated into one via AM, 25% weight reduction, over 100,000 units produced annually)
  • Medical implants: Titanium hip implants and dental implants (optimized to patient-specific anatomy, porous structures that promote osseointegration)
  • Custom products: Hearing aids (over 10 million units manufactured annually via AM), athletic shoe midsoles (Adidas 4D, Carbon's DLS technology)
  • Spare parts: On-demand manufacturing of discontinued or rare parts (automotive, aerospace, industrial machinery)
⚠️ Constraints and Challenges of AM

AM is not a universal solution and has the following constraints:

  • Build speed: Not suited for mass production (injection molding: 1 part/few seconds vs. AM: hours). The economic break-even point is typically under 1,000 units
  • Build volume limits: Parts larger than the build volume (typically around 200×200×200mm on many machines) must be manufactured in sections
  • Surface quality: Layer lines remain visible, so post-processing (polishing, machining) is required when high-precision surfaces are needed
  • Material property anisotropy: Mechanical properties can differ between the build direction (Z-axis) and the in-plane direction (XY plane), especially with FDM
  • Material cost: AM-grade materials are 2-10 times more expensive than general-purpose materials (though this can be offset by material efficiency and design optimization)

1.2.2 Material Extrusion (MEX)

Principle: A thermoplastic filament is heated, melted, and extruded through a nozzle, then deposited layer by layer. This is the most widely used AM technology (also called FDM/FFF).

Process: filament → heated nozzle (190-260°C) → melt extrusion → cooling and solidification → next layer deposition

Characteristics:

  • Low cost: Equipment price $200-$5,000 (desktop), $10,000-$100,000 (industrial)
  • Material diversity: PLA, ABS, PETG, nylon, PC, carbon fiber composites, PEEK (high-performance)
  • Build speed: 20-150 mm³/s (moderate), layer height 0.1-0.4mm
  • Precision: ±0.2-0.5 mm (desktop), ±0.1 mm (industrial)
  • Surface quality: Layer lines clearly visible (can be improved with post-processing)
  • Material anisotropy: Z-axis (build direction) strength is 20-80% lower (weak inter-layer bonding)

Application examples:

  • Prototyping (the most common use, low cost and fast)
  • Jigs and tools (used on the shop floor, lightweight and easily customized)
  • Educational models (widely used in schools and universities, safe and low cost)
  • End-use parts (custom hearing aids, prosthetics and orthotics, architectural models)
💡 Representative FDM Machines
  • Ultimaker S5: Dual head, 330×240×300mm build volume, $6,000
  • Prusa i3 MK4: Open-source based, highly reliable, $1,200
  • Stratasys Fortus 450mc: Industrial grade, supports ULTEM 9085, $250,000
  • Markforged X7: Supports continuous carbon fiber composites, $100,000

1.2.3 Vat Photopolymerization (VPP)

Principle: A liquid photocurable resin (photopolymer) is selectively cured layer by layer using a UV laser or projector.

Process: UV exposure → photopolymerization reaction → solidification → build platform rises → next layer exposure

The two main VPP methods:

  1. SLA (Stereolithography): A UV laser (355 nm) is scanned by galvo mirrors, curing the resin point by point. High precision but slow.
  2. DLP (Digital Light Processing): A projector exposes an entire layer at once. Fast, but resolution depends on the projector's pixel count (Full HD: 1920×1080).
  3. LCD-MSLA (Masked SLA): Uses an LCD mask, similar to DLP but lower cost (many desktop machines in the $200-$1,000 range).

Characteristics:

  • High precision: XY resolution 25-100 μm, Z resolution 10-50 μm (among the highest of all AM technologies)
  • Surface quality: Smooth surface (Ra < 5 μm), layer lines nearly invisible
  • Build speed: SLA (10-50 mm³/s), DLP/LCD (100-500 mm³/s, area-dependent)
  • Material constraints: Photocurable resins only (mechanical properties are often inferior to FDM)
  • Post-processing required: washing (IPA, etc.) → post-curing (UV exposure) → support removal

Application examples:

  • Dental applications (orthodontic models, surgical guides, dentures; millions of units produced annually)
  • Wax models for jewelry casting (high precision, complex geometry)
  • Medical models (surgical planning, anatomical models, patient education)
  • Master models (for silicone molding, design verification)

1.2.4 Powder Bed Fusion (PBF)

Principle: A thin layer of powder material is spread, then selectively melted or sintered with a laser or electron beam and solidified layer by layer. Applicable to metals, polymers, and ceramics.

Process: powder spreading → laser/electron beam scanning → melting/sintering → solidification → next layer of powder spreading

The three main PBF methods:

  1. SLS (Selective Laser Sintering): Sinters polymer powder (e.g., PA12 nylon) with a laser. No support needed (surrounding powder provides support).
  2. SLM (Selective Laser Melting): Fully melts metal powder (Ti-6Al-4V, AlSi10Mg, Inconel 718, etc.). Capable of producing high-density parts (relative density >99%).
  3. EBM (Electron Beam Melting): Melts metal powder with an electron beam. High-temperature preheating (650-1000°C) results in low residual stress and faster build speed.

Characteristics:

  • High strength: Melting and re-solidification produces mechanical properties comparable to forged material (tensile strength 500-1200 MPa)
  • Complex geometry capability: No support needed (powder provides support), enabling overhang builds
  • Material diversity: Ti alloys, Al alloys, stainless steel, Ni superalloys, Co-Cr alloys, nylon
  • High cost: Equipment price $200,000-$1,500,000, material cost $50-$500/kg
  • Post-processing: support removal, heat treatment (stress relief), surface finishing (blasting, polishing)

Application examples:

  • Aerospace components (weight reduction, part consolidation, e.g., GE LEAP fuel nozzles)
  • Medical implants (patient-specific geometry, porous structures, Ti-6Al-4V)
  • Molds (conformal cooling, complex geometry, H13 tool steel)
  • Automotive parts (lightweight brackets, custom engine components)

1.2.5 Material Jetting (MJ)

Principle: Similar to inkjet printing, droplets of material (photocurable resin or wax) are jetted from a print head and instantly cured with UV light, layer by layer.

Characteristics:

  • Ultra-high precision: XY resolution 42-85 μm, Z resolution 16-32 μm
  • Multi-material: Multiple materials and colors can be combined within a single build
  • Full-color builds: Combining CMYK resins allows for more than 10 million colors
  • Surface quality: Extremely smooth (almost no visible layer lines)
  • High cost: Equipment $50,000-$300,000, material cost $200-$600/kg
  • Material constraints: Photocurable resins only, moderate mechanical properties

Application examples:: Medical anatomical models (soft and hard tissue reproduced with different materials), full-color architectural models, design verification models

1.2.6 Binder Jetting (BJ)

Principle: A liquid binder (adhesive) is jetted onto a powder bed using an inkjet method to bond powder particles together. Strength is increased after the build via sintering or infiltration.

Characteristics:

  • High-speed build: No laser scanning required, entire layers processed at once, build speed 100-500 mm³/s
  • Material diversity: Metal powders, ceramics, sand molds (for casting), full-color (gypsum)
  • No support needed: Surrounding powder provides support and can be recycled after removal
  • Low-density issue: Fragile before sintering (green density 50-60%), relative density of 90-98% even after sintering
  • Post-processing required: debinding → sintering (metals: 1200-1400°C) → infiltration (copper/bronze)

Application examples:: Sand molds for casting (large castings such as engine blocks), metal parts (Desktop Metal, HP Metal Jet), full-color figures (memorabilia, educational models)

1.2.7 Sheet Lamination (SL)

Principle: Sheet materials (paper, metal foil, plastic film) are stacked and bonded or welded together, with each layer cut to contour by laser or blade.

Representative technologies:

  • LOM (Laminated Object Manufacturing): Paper or plastic sheets bonded with adhesive and laser-cut
  • UAM (Ultrasonic Additive Manufacturing): Metal foils joined by ultrasonic welding, contoured with CNC machining

Characteristics: Capable of large builds, low material cost, moderate precision, limited applications (mainly visual models; embedded sensors for metal variants)

1.2.8 Directed Energy Deposition (DED)

Principle: Metal powder or wire is fed while being melted by a laser, electron beam, or arc, and deposited onto a substrate. Used for large parts and repair of existing components.

Characteristics:

  • High deposition rate: 1-5 kg/h (10-50 times that of PBF)
  • Large-scale capability: Fewer build volume constraints (multi-axis robotic arms are used)
  • Repair and coating: Repairs worn areas of existing parts, forms hardened surface layers
  • Lower precision: Precision ±0.5-2 mm, post-processing (machining) required

Application examples:: Turbine blade repair, large aerospace components, wear-resistant tool coatings

⚠️ Guidance for Process Selection

The optimal AM process depends on application requirements:

  • Precision is the top priority → VPP (SLA/DLP) or MJ
  • Low cost, widespread adoption → MEX (FDM/FFF)
  • High-strength metal parts → PBF (SLM/EBM)
  • Mass production (sand molds) → BJ
  • Large scale, high-speed deposition → DED

1.3 The STL File Format and Data Processing

1.3.1 Structure of the STL File

STL (STereoLithography) is the most widely used 3D model file format in AM, developed by 3D Systems in 1987. STL files represent the surface of an object as a collection of triangular meshes.

Basic Structure of an STL File

STL file = normal vector (n) + 3 vertex coordinates (v1, v2, v3) × number of triangles

Example of ASCII STL format:

solid cube
  facet normal 0 0 1
    outer loop
      vertex 0 0 10
      vertex 10 0 10
      vertex 10 10 10
    endloop
  endfacet
  facet normal 0 0 1
    outer loop
      vertex 0 0 10
      vertex 10 10 10
      vertex 0 10 10
    endloop
  endfacet
  ...
endsolid cube

The two types of STL format:

  1. ASCII STL: A human-readable text format. Large file size (10-20 times larger than binary for the same model). Useful for debugging and verification.
  2. Binary STL: A binary format, small file size, fast processing. The industry standard. Structure: 80-byte header + 4-byte triangle count + 50 bytes per triangle (12B normal + 36B vertices + 2B attribute).

1.3.2 Key Concepts in STL Files

1. Normal Vector

Each triangular face has a defined normal vector (outward direction) that distinguishes the "inside" from the "outside" of the object. The normal direction is determined by the right-hand rule:

normal n = (v2 - v1) × (v3 - v1) / |(v2 - v1) × (v3 - v1)|

Vertex order rule: Vertices v1, v2, v3 are arranged counter-clockwise (CCW), and when viewed from outside in counter-clockwise order, the normal points outward.

2. Manifold Conditions

For an STL mesh to be printable, it must be manifold:

  • Edge sharing: Every edge is shared by exactly two triangles
  • Vertex sharing: Every vertex belongs to a continuous triangle fan
  • Closed surface: No holes or openings; the surface forms a fully closed shape
  • No self-intersection: Triangles do not intersect or penetrate each other
⚠️ Problems with Non-Manifold Meshes

Non-manifold meshes cannot be 3D printed. Typical problems include:

  • Holes: Unclosed surfaces where an edge belongs to only one triangle
  • T-junctions: An edge shared by three or more triangles
  • Inverted normals: A mix of triangles whose normals point inward
  • Duplicate vertices: Multiple vertices existing at the same location
  • Degenerate triangles: Triangles with zero or near-zero area

These problems cause errors in slicing software and result in failed builds.

1.3.3 STL File Quality Metrics

The quality of an STL mesh is evaluated using the following metrics:

  1. Triangle count: Typically 10,000-500,000. Avoid too few (coarse model) or too many (large file size, slow processing).
  2. Edge length uniformity: A mix of extremely large and small triangles degrades build quality. Ideally in the range of 0.1-1.0 mm.
  3. Aspect ratio: Long, thin triangles (high aspect ratio) cause numerical errors. Ideally aspect ratio < 10.
  4. Normal consistency: All normals should be consistently outward-facing. Mixed inverted normals cause inside/outside determination errors.
💡 STL File Resolution Trade-off

STL mesh resolution (triangle count) is a trade-off between accuracy and file size:

  • Low resolution (1,000-10,000 triangles): Fast processing, small file, but curved surfaces appear faceted
  • Medium resolution (10,000-100,000 triangles): Appropriate for most applications, good balance
  • High resolution (100,000-1,000,000 triangles): Smooth curved surfaces, but large file size (tens of MB) and slower processing

When exporting STL from CAD software, resolution is controlled via chordal tolerance or angle tolerance. Recommended values: chordal tolerance 0.01-0.1 mm, angle tolerance 5-15 degrees.

1.3.4 STL Processing with Python Libraries

Key Python libraries for working with STL files:

  1. numpy-stl: Fast STL reading/writing, volume and surface area calculation, normal vector manipulation. Simple and lightweight.
  2. trimesh: A comprehensive 3D mesh processing library. Mesh repair, boolean operations, ray casting, collision detection. Feature-rich but with many dependencies.
  3. PyMesh: Advanced mesh processing (remeshing, subdivision, feature extraction). Somewhat complex to install.

Basic usage of numpy-stl:

from stl import mesh
import numpy as np

# Load an STL file
your_mesh = mesh.Mesh.from_file('model.stl')

# Basic geometric information

print(f"Volume: {volume:.2f} mm³")
print(f"Center of Gravity: {cog}")
print(f"Surface Area: {your_mesh.areas.sum():.2f} mm²")

# Triangle count
print(f"Number of Triangles: {len(your_mesh.vectors)}")

1.4 Slicing and Toolpath Generation

The process of converting an STL file into instructions (G-code) that a 3D printer can understand is called slicing. In this section, we will learn the basic principles of slicing, toolpath strategies, and the fundamentals of G-code.

Choosing Layer Height

Layer height is the most important parameter determining the trade-off between build quality and build time:

Layer Height Build Quality Build Time Typical Applications
0.1 mm (very fine) Very high (layer lines nearly invisible) Very long (×2-3) Figurines, medical models, end-use parts
0.2 mm (standard) Good (layer lines visible but acceptable) Standard General prototypes, functional parts
0.3 mm (coarse) Low (layer lines pronounced) Short (×0.5) Early-stage prototypes, internal structural parts
⚠️ Layer Height Constraints

Layer height must be set to 25-80% of the nozzle diameter. For example, with a 0.4mm nozzle, the recommended layer height range is 0.1-0.32mm. Exceeding this can cause insufficient extrusion or the nozzle dragging on the previous layer.

1.4.2 Shell and Infill Strategies

Generating the Shell

The shell (shell/perimeter) is the path that forms the outer boundary of each layer:

  • Shell count (perimeter count): Typically 2-4. Affects external quality and strength.
    • 1 shell: Very weak, high transparency, decorative use only
    • 2 shells: Standard (good balance)
    • 3-4 shells: High strength, improved surface quality, improved airtightness
  • Shell order: Inside-out is common. Outside-in is used when surface quality is the priority.

Infill Patterns

Infill forms the internal structure and controls strength and material usage:

Pattern Strength Print Speed Material Usage Characteristics
Grid Medium Fast Medium Simple, isotropic, standard choice
Honeycomb High Slow Medium High strength, excellent strength-to-weight ratio, aerospace use
Gyroid Very High Medium Medium 3D isotropic, curved geometry, latest recommendation
Concentric Low Fast Low Flexibility-focused, follows the shell contour
Lines Low (anisotropic) Very fast Low High-speed printing, directional strength
💡 Guideline for Infill Density
  • 0-10%: Decorative items, non-load-bearing parts (prioritizing material savings)
  • 20%: Standard prototypes (good balance)
  • 40-60%: Functional parts, high-strength requirements
  • 100%: End-use parts, watertightness requirements, maximum strength (build time ×3-5)

1.4.3 Generating Support Structures

Areas with an overhang angle exceeding 45 degrees require support structures:

Types of Supports

  • Linear support: Vertical, column-like supports. Simple and easy to remove but uses more material.
  • Tree support: Branching, tree-like supports. Reduces material usage by 30-50% and is easier to remove. The default support type in Cura and PrusaSlicer.
  • Interface layers: A thin bonding layer placed on top of the support. Easier to remove and improves surface quality. Typically 2-4 layers.

Key Support Settings

Parameter Recommended Value Effect
Overhang Angle 45-60° Supports are generated beyond this angle
Support Density 10-20% Higher density is more stable but harder to remove
Support Z Distance 0.2-0.3 mm Gap between the support and the model (affects ease of removal)
Interface Layers 2-4 layers Number of bonding layers (balances surface quality and removability)

1.4.6 Toolpath Optimization Strategies

Efficient toolpaths improve build time, quality, and material usage:

  • Retraction: Pulls the filament back during travel moves to prevent stringing.
    • Distance: 1-6mm (Bowden-style: 4-6mm, direct-drive: 1-2mm)
    • Speed: 25-45 mm/s
    • Excessive retraction can cause nozzle clogs
  • Z-hop: Raises the nozzle during travel moves to avoid collisions with the model. 0.2-0.5mm rise. Slightly increases build time but improves surface quality.
  • Combing: Restricts travel paths to stay above infill, reducing travel marks on the surface. Effective when appearance matters.
  • Seam position: A strategy for aligning the start/end point of each layer.
    • Random: random placement (less noticeable)
    • Aligned: placed in a straight line (easier to remove the seam via post-processing)
    • Sharpest corner: placed at the sharpest corner (less noticeable)

# Simple build-time estimate (assuming a 0.2mm layer height and 50mm/s print speed)

import numpy as np
from stl import mesh

def check_normals(mesh_data):
    """Check the consistency of an STL mesh's normal vectors

    Args:
        mesh_data: a numpy-stl Mesh object

    Returns:
        tuple: (flipped_count, total_count, percentage)
    """
    # Verify normal direction using the right-hand rule
    flipped_count = 0
    total_count = len(mesh_data.vectors)

    for i, facet in enumerate(mesh_data.vectors):
        v0, v1, v2 = facet

        # Compute edge vectors
        edge1 = v1 - v0
        edge2 = v2 - v0

        # Compute the normal via cross product (right-handed)
        calculated_normal = np.cross(edge1, edge2)

        # Normalize
        norm = np.linalg.norm(calculated_normal)
        if norm > 1e-10:  # confirm it's not a zero vector
            calculated_normal = calculated_normal / norm
        else:
            continue  # skip degenerate triangles

        # Compare against the normal stored in the file
        stored_normal = mesh_data.normals[i]
        stored_norm = np.linalg.norm(stored_normal)

        if stored_norm > 1e-10:
            stored_normal = stored_normal / stored_norm

        # Check direction agreement via dot product
        dot_product = np.dot(calculated_normal, stored_normal)

        # A negative dot product means the normal is reversed
        if dot_product < 0:
            flipped_count += 1

    percentage = (flipped_count / total_count) * 100 if total_count > 0 else 0

    return flipped_count, total_count, percentage

import numpy as np

def slice_mesh_at_height(mesh_data, z_height):
    """Generate a temperature profile

    Args:
        t (array): time array [min]
        T_target (float): hold temperature [°C]
        heating_rate (float): heating rate [°C/min]
        hold_time (float): hold time [min]
        cooling_rate (float): cooling rate [°C/min]

    Returns:
        array: temperature profile [°C]
    """
    T_room = 25  # room temperature
    T = np.zeros_like(t)

    # Heating duration
    t_heat = (T_target - T_room) / heating_rate

    # Cooling start time
    t_cool_start = t_heat + hold_time

    for i, time in enumerate(t):
        if time <= t_heat:
            # Heating phase
            T[i] = T_room + heating_rate * time
        elif time <= t_cool_start:
            # Hold phase
            T[i] = T_target
        else:
            # Cooling phase
            T[i] = T_target - cooling_rate * (time - t_cool_start)
            T[i] = max(T[i], T_room)  # never drops below room temperature

    return T

def simulate_reaction_progress(T, t, Ea, D0, r0):
    """Calculate reaction progress based on a temperature profile

    Args:
        T (array): temperature profile [°C]
        t (array): time array [min]
        Ea (float): activation energy [J/mol]
        D0 (float): pre-exponential factor [m²/s]
        r0 (float): particle radius [m]

    Returns:
        array: conversion fraction
    """
    R = 8.314
    C0 = 10000
    alpha = np.zeros_like(t)

    for i in range(1, len(t)):
        T_k = T[i] + 273.15
        D = D0 * np.exp(-Ea / (R * T_k))
        k = D * C0 / r0**2

        dt = (t[i] - t[i-1]) * 60  # min → s

        # Simple integration (reaction progress over a small time step)
        if alpha[i-1] < 0.99:
            dalpha = k * dt / (2 * (1 - (1-alpha[i-1])**(1/3)))
            alpha[i] = min(alpha[i-1] + dalpha, 1.0)
        else:
            alpha[i] = alpha[i-1]

    return alpha

import numpy as np

# Experimental data (time vs. conversion)
# Example: BaTiO3 synthesis @ 1200°C
time_exp = np.array([0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20])  # hours
conversion_exp = np.array([0, 0.15, 0.28, 0.38, 0.47, 0.60,
                          0.70, 0.78, 0.84, 0.90, 0.95])

# Jander model
def jander_model(t, k):
    """Calculate conversion using the Jander equation

    Args:
        t (array): time [hours]
        k (float): rate constant

    Returns:
        array: conversion
    """
    # Solve [1 - (1-α)^(1/3)]² = kt for α
    kt = k * t
    alpha = 1 - (1 - np.sqrt(kt))**3
    alpha = np.clip(alpha, 0, 1)  # constrain to the 0-1 range
    return alpha

# Ginstling-Brounshtein model (an alternative diffusion model)
def gb_model(t, k):
    """Ginstling-Brounshtein equation

    Args:
        t (array): time
        k (float): rate constant

    Returns:
        array: conversion
    """
    # 1 - 2α/3 - (1-α)^(2/3) = kt
    # This should be solved numerically, but an approximation is used here
    kt = k * t
    alpha = 1 - (1 - kt/2)**(3/2)
    alpha = np.clip(alpha, 0, 1)
    return alpha

# Power law (empirical model)
def power_law_model(t, k, n):
    """Power law model

    Args:
        t (array): time
        k (float): rate constant
        n (float): exponent

    Returns:
        array: conversion
    """
    alpha = k * t**n
    alpha = np.clip(alpha, 0, 1)
    return alpha

# Fit each model
# Jander equation

k_jander = popt_jander[0]

k_gb = popt_gb[0]

    ss_res = np.sum((y_true - y_pred)**2)
    ss_tot = np.sum((y_true - np.mean(y_true))**2)
    return 1 - (ss_res / ss_tot)

1.8 Advanced Topic: Microstructure Control

1.8.1 Suppressing Grain Growth

In solid-state reactions, undesirable grain growth occurs due to high-temperature, long-duration holds. Strategies to suppress this include:

  • Two-step sintering: a short hold at high temperature followed by a long hold at low temperature
  • Use of additives: adding trace amounts of grain-growth inhibitors (e.g., MgO, Al₂O₃)
  • Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS): rapid heating and short-duration sintering

1.8.2 Mechanochemical Activation of Reactions

Mechanochemical methods (high-energy ball milling) can also drive solid-state reactions forward near room temperature:

# ===================================
# Example 8: Grain growth simulation
# ===================================

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def grain_growth(t, T, D0, Ea, G0, n):
    """Time evolution of grain growth

    Burke-Turnbull equation: G^n - G0^n = k*t

    Args:
        t (array): time [hours]
        T (float): temperature [K]
        D0 (float): pre-exponential factor
        Ea (float): activation energy [J/mol]
        G0 (float): initial grain size [μm]
        n (float): grain growth exponent (typically 2-4)

    Returns:
        array: grain size [μm]
    """
    R = 8.314
    k = D0 * np.exp(-Ea / (R * T))
    G = (G0**n + k * t * 3600)**(1/n)  # hours → seconds
    return G

# Parameter settings
D0_grain = 1e8  # μm^n/s
Ea_grain = 400e3  # J/mol
G0 = 0.5  # μm
n = 3

# Effect of temperature
temps_celsius = [1100, 1200, 1300]
t_range = np.linspace(0, 12, 100)  # 0-12 hours

plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))

# Temperature dependence
plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
for T_c in temps_celsius:
    T_k = T_c + 273.15
    G = grain_growth(t_range, T_k, D0_grain, Ea_grain, G0, n)
    plt.plot(t_range, G, linewidth=2, label=f'{T_c}°C')

plt.axhline(y=1.0, color='red', linestyle='--', linewidth=1,
            label='Target grain size')

Explanation:
