1.1 What Is Additive Manufacturing (AM)?

1.1.1 Definition of Additive Manufacturing

Additive Manufacturing (AM) is defined by the ISO/ASTM 52900:2021 standard as "the process of manufacturing objects from 3D CAD data by joining materials layer by layer." In contrast to conventional subtractive machining (material removal), AM adds material only where needed, giving it the following innovative characteristics:

  • Design freedom: Enables manufacturing of complex geometries impossible with conventional methods (hollow structures, lattice structures, topology-optimized shapes)
  • Material efficiency: Since material is used only where needed, waste is just 5-10% (compared to 30-90% waste for conventional machining)
  • On-demand manufacturing: Enables low-volume, high-mix production of customized products without tooling
  • Consolidated manufacturing: Builds structures as a single piece that traditionally required assembling multiple parts, eliminating assembly steps
💡 Industrial Significance

The AM market is growing rapidly. According to the Wohlers Report 2023:

  • Global AM market size: $18.3B (2023) → projected $83.9B (2030, 23.5% CAGR)
  • Application breakdown: prototyping (38%), tooling (27%), end-use parts (35%)
  • Major industries: aerospace (26%), medical (21%), automotive (18%), consumer goods (15%)
  • Material share: polymers (55%), metals (35%), ceramics (7%), other (3%)

1.1.3 Major Application Areas of AM

Application 1: Rapid Prototyping

AM's first major use case: quickly manufacturing prototypes for design verification, functional testing, and market evaluation:

  • Shorter lead times: from weeks-to-months for conventional prototyping to hours-to-days with AM
  • Faster design iteration: producing multiple versions inexpensively to optimize the design
  • Improved communication: visual and tactile physical models help align stakeholders
  • Typical examples: automotive design models, appliance housing prototypes, pre-surgical simulation models for medical devices

Application 2: Tooling & Fixtures

Using AM to manufacture jigs, tools, and molds used on the shop floor:

  • Custom fixtures: rapidly producing assembly and inspection fixtures tailored to a production line
  • Conformal cooling molds: injection molds with 3D cooling channels that follow the product shape rather than conventional straight channels (30-70% shorter cooling time)
  • Lightweight tooling: lightweight end effectors using lattice structures to reduce operator strain
  • Typical examples: BMW's assembly-line fixtures (over 100,000 produced annually with AM), TaylorMade golf driver molds

Application 3: End-Use Parts

Directly manufacturing final products with AM has surged in recent years:

  • Aerospace components: GE Aviation's LEAP fuel injection nozzle (consolidated from 20 conventional parts into a single AM part, 25% lighter, over 100,000 produced annually)
  • Medical implants: titanium hip implants and dental implants (optimized to a patient's specific anatomy, with porous structures that promote osseointegration)
  • Custom products: hearing aids (over 10 million produced annually with AM), athletic shoe midsoles (Adidas 4D, Carbon's DLS technology)
  • Spare parts: on-demand production of discontinued or rare parts (automotive, aerospace, industrial machinery)
⚠️ Limitations and Challenges of AM

AM is not a universal solution and has the following limitations:

  • Build speed: not suited to mass production (injection molding: one part per few seconds vs. AM: hours per part). The economic break-even point is typically below 1,000 units
  • Build size limits: parts larger than the build volume (roughly 200×200×200 mm on many machines) require splitting and joining
  • Surface quality: visible layer lines require post-processing (polishing, machining) when a high-precision surface finish is needed
  • Anisotropic material properties: mechanical properties can differ between the build direction (Z-axis) and the in-plane direction (XY plane), especially with FDM
  • Material cost: AM-grade materials cost 2-10× more than general-purpose materials (though this can be offset by material efficiency and design optimization)

1.2.2 Material Extrusion (MEX)

Principle: A thermoplastic filament is heated and melted, then extruded through a nozzle and deposited layer by layer. This is the most widespread AM technology (also known as FDM/FFF).

Process: Filament → heated nozzle (190-260°C) → molten extrusion → cooling/solidification → next layer deposited

Characteristics:

  • Low cost: machine price $200-$5,000 (desktop), $10,000-$100,000 (industrial)
  • Material diversity: PLA, ABS, PETG, nylon, PC, carbon-fiber composites, PEEK (high-performance)
  • Build speed: 20-150 mm³/s (moderate), layer height 0.1-0.4 mm
  • Accuracy: ±0.2-0.5 mm (desktop), ±0.1 mm (industrial)
  • Surface quality: visible layer lines (can be improved with post-processing)
  • Material anisotropy: Z-axis (build direction) strength is 20-80% lower — interlayer adhesion is the weak point

Applications:

  • Prototyping (the most common use case — low cost and fast)
  • Jigs and tools (used on the shop floor — lightweight and easily customized)
  • Educational models (widely used in schools and universities — safe and low cost)
  • End-use parts (custom hearing aids, prosthetics, architectural models)
💡 Representative FDM Machines
  • Ultimaker S5: dual print head, build volume 330×240×300 mm, $6,000
  • Prusa i3 MK4: open-source lineage, highly reliable, $1,200
  • Stratasys Fortus 450mc: industrial, supports ULTEM 9085, $250,000
  • Markforged X7: supports continuous carbon-fiber composites, $100,000

1.2.3 Vat Photopolymerization (VPP)

Principle: A liquid photopolymer resin is selectively cured with a UV laser or projector to build up layers.

Process: UV exposure → photopolymerization reaction → solidification → build platform rises → next layer exposed

Two main VPP approaches:

  1. SLA (Stereolithography): a UV laser (355 nm) scanned by a galvanometer mirror cures the resin point by point. High precision but slow.
  2. DLP (Digital Light Processing): a projector exposes an entire layer at once. Fast, but resolution is limited by the projector's pixel count (Full HD: 1920×1080).
  3. LCD-MSLA (Masked SLA): uses an LCD mask; similar to DLP but lower cost (many desktop machines in the $200-$1,000 range).

Characteristics:

  • High precision: XY resolution 25-100 μm, Z resolution 10-50 μm (among the highest of all AM technologies)
  • Surface quality: smooth surface (Ra < 5 μm), layer lines barely visible
  • Build speed: SLA (10-50 mm³/s), DLP/LCD (100-500 mm³/s, area dependent)
  • Material constraints: photopolymer resins only (mechanical properties are often inferior to FDM)
  • Post-processing required: washing (IPA, etc.) → secondary curing (UV exposure) → support removal

Applications:

  • Dental applications (orthodontic models, surgical guides, dentures — millions produced annually)
  • Wax models for jewelry casting (high precision, complex geometry)
  • Medical models (pre-surgical planning, anatomical models, patient education)
  • Master models (for silicone molding, design verification)

1.2.4 Powder Bed Fusion (PBF)

Principle: A thin layer of powder is spread, then selectively melted or sintered with a laser or electron beam and allowed to solidify, layer by layer. Applicable to metals, polymers, and ceramics.

Process: powder spreading → laser/electron-beam scanning → melting/sintering → solidification → next layer spread

Three main PBF approaches:

  1. SLS (Selective Laser Sintering): sinters polymer powder (e.g., PA12 nylon) with a laser. No support needed — the surrounding powder provides support.
  2. SLM (Selective Laser Melting): fully melts metal powder (Ti-6Al-4V, AlSi10Mg, Inconel 718, etc.), producing high-density parts (relative density >99%).
  3. EBM (Electron Beam Melting): melts metal powder with an electron beam. High-temperature preheating (650-1000°C) reduces residual stress and increases build speed.

Characteristics:

  • High strength: melting and re-solidification produces mechanical properties comparable to forged material (tensile strength 500-1200 MPa)
  • Complex geometry: no supports needed (powder provides support), enabling overhang builds
  • Material diversity: Ti alloys, Al alloys, stainless steel, Ni superalloys, Co-Cr alloys, nylon
  • High cost: machine price $200,000-$1,500,000, material cost $50-$500/kg
  • Post-processing: support removal, heat treatment (stress relief), surface finishing (blasting, polishing)

Applications:

  • Aerospace components (weight reduction, part consolidation, GE LEAP fuel nozzles, etc.)
  • Medical implants (patient-specific geometry, porous structures, Ti-6Al-4V)
  • Molds and dies (conformal cooling, complex shapes, H13 tool steel)
  • Automotive parts (lightweight brackets, custom engine components)

1.2.5 Material Jetting (MJ)

Principle: Similar to an inkjet printer — droplets of material (photopolymer resin or wax) are jetted from a print head and immediately cured with UV light, layer by layer.

Characteristics:

  • Ultra-high precision: XY resolution 42-85 μm, Z resolution 16-32 μm
  • Multi-material: multiple materials and colors can be combined within a single build
  • Full-color builds: over 10 million colors achievable by combining CMYK resins
  • Surface quality: extremely smooth (nearly no visible layer lines)
  • High cost: machine $50,000-$300,000, material cost $200-$600/kg
  • Material constraints: photopolymer resins only, moderate mechanical properties

Applications: medical anatomical models (soft and hard tissue reproduced with different materials), full-color architectural models, design verification models

1.2.6 Binder Jetting (BJ)

Principle: A liquid binder is jetted onto a powder bed to bond powder particles, using inkjet-style deposition. After building, sintering or infiltration strengthens the part.

Characteristics:

  • High-speed build: no laser scanning needed — an entire layer is processed at once; build speed 100-500 mm³/s
  • Material diversity: metal powders, ceramics, sand (for casting molds), full color (gypsum)
  • No supports needed: surrounding powder provides support and can be recycled after removal
  • Low-density issue: fragile before sintering (green density 50-60%); even after sintering, relative density is only 90-98%
  • Post-processing required: debinding → sintering (metal: 1200-1400°C) → infiltration (copper, bronze)

Applications: sand-casting molds (large castings such as engine blocks), metal parts (Desktop Metal, HP Metal Jet), full-color figurines (commemorative items, educational models)

1.2.7 Sheet Lamination (SL)

Principle: Sheet materials (paper, metal foil, plastic film) are stacked and bonded or welded together, with each layer's contour cut by a laser or blade.

Representative technologies:

  • LOM (Laminated Object Manufacturing): paper or plastic sheets bonded with adhesive, contours cut with a laser
  • UAM (Ultrasonic Additive Manufacturing): metal foils joined by ultrasonic welding, contours machined with CNC

Characteristics: capable of large-scale builds, inexpensive materials, moderate precision, limited applications (mainly visual models; embedded sensors for metal variants)

1.2.8 Directed Energy Deposition (DED)

Principle: Metal powder or wire is fed and melted with a laser, electron beam, or arc, and deposited onto a substrate. Used for large parts and repair of existing parts.

Characteristics:

  • High deposition rate: 1-5 kg/h (10-50× faster than PBF)
  • Large-scale capability: few build-volume constraints (multi-axis robotic arms are used)
  • Repair and coating: repairing worn areas of existing parts, forming hardened surface layers
  • Lower precision: accuracy ±0.5-2 mm; post-processing (machining) required

Applications: turbine blade repair, large aerospace components, wear-resistant tool coatings

⚠️ Guidelines for Process Selection

The optimal AM process depends on application requirements:

  • Precision is the top priority → VPP (SLA/DLP) or MJ
  • Low cost, widespread availability → MEX (FDM/FFF)
  • High-strength metal parts → PBF (SLM/EBM)
  • Mass production (sand molds) → BJ
  • Large scale, high-speed deposition → DED

1.3 STL File Format and Data Processing

1.3.1 Structure of STL Files

STL (STereoLithography) is the most widely used 3D model file format for AM, developed by 3D Systems in 1987. STL files represent an object's surface as a collection of triangle meshes.

Basic Structure of an STL File

STL file = normal vector (n) + 3 vertex coordinates (v1, v2, v3) × number of triangles

Example of ASCII STL format:

  facet normal 0 0 1
    outer loop
      vertex 0 0 10
      vertex 10 0 10
      vertex 10 10 10
    endloop
  endfacet
  facet normal 0 0 1
    outer loop
      vertex 0 0 10
      vertex 10 10 10
      vertex 0 10 10
    endloop
  endfacet
  ...
endsolid cube

Two types of STL format:

  1. ASCII STL: human-readable text format. Large file size (10-20× larger than the equivalent binary file for the same model). Useful for debugging and verification.
  2. Binary STL: binary format, small file size, fast to process. The industrial standard. Structure: 80-byte header + 4-byte triangle count + 50 bytes per triangle (12B normal + 36B vertices + 2B attribute).

1.3.2 Key Concepts of STL Files

1. Normal Vector

Each triangular facet has a defined normal vector (outward-facing direction) that distinguishes the "inside" from the "outside" of the object. The normal direction is determined by the right-hand rule:

Normal n = (v2 - v1) × (v3 - v1) / |(v2 - v1) × (v3 - v1)|

Vertex ordering rule: Vertices v1, v2, v3 are arranged counter-clockwise (CCW), so that when viewed from outside, the counter-clockwise vertex order produces an outward-facing normal.

2. Manifold Condition

For an STL mesh to be 3D printable, it must be manifold:

  • Edge sharing: every edge is shared by exactly two triangles
  • Vertex sharing: every vertex belongs to a continuous triangle fan
  • Closed surface: no holes or openings — the surface must be fully enclosed
  • No self-intersection: triangles do not intersect or pass through one another
⚠️ Problems with Non-Manifold Meshes

A non-manifold mesh cannot be 3D printed. Typical problems include:

  • Holes: an unclosed surface where an edge belongs to only one triangle
  • T-junctions: an edge shared by three or more triangles
  • Inverted normals: some triangles have normals facing inward, mixed in with correctly oriented ones
  • Duplicate vertices: multiple vertices existing at the same position
  • Degenerate triangles: triangles with zero or near-zero area

These problems cause errors in slicer software and lead to failed prints.

1.3.3 STL File Quality Metrics

STL mesh quality is evaluated using the following metrics:

  1. Triangle count: typically 10,000-500,000. Too few (coarse model) or too many (large file size, slow processing) should be avoided.
  2. Edge-length uniformity: a mix of extremely large and small triangles degrades build quality. Ideally in the 0.1-1.0 mm range.
  3. Aspect ratio: long, thin triangles (high aspect ratio) cause numerical errors. Ideally, aspect ratio < 10.
  4. Normal consistency: all normals should be uniformly outward-facing. Mixed-in inverted normals cause inside/outside detection errors.
💡 STL Resolution Trade-off

STL mesh resolution (triangle count) is a trade-off between accuracy and file size:

  • Low resolution (1,000-10,000 triangles): fast processing, small file, but curved surfaces appear faceted
  • Medium resolution (10,000-100,000 triangles): appropriate for most applications, a good balance
  • High resolution (100,000-1,000,000 triangles): smooth curved surfaces, but large file size (tens of MB) and slow processing

When exporting an STL from CAD software, resolution is controlled by chordal tolerance or angular tolerance. Recommended values: chordal tolerance 0.01-0.1 mm, angular tolerance 5-15 degrees.

1.3.4 STL Processing with Python Libraries

The main Python libraries for working with STL files:

  1. numpy-stl: fast STL reading/writing, volume and surface-area calculation, normal-vector manipulation. Simple and lightweight.
  2. trimesh: a comprehensive 3D mesh processing library. Mesh repair, boolean operations, raycasting, collision detection. Feature-rich but with many dependencies.
  3. PyMesh: advanced mesh processing (remeshing, subdivision, feature extraction). Somewhat more complex to install.

Basic usage of numpy-stl:

from stl import mesh
import numpy as np

# Load an STL file
your_mesh = mesh.Mesh.from_file('model.stl')

# Basic geometric information
volume, cog, inertia = your_mesh.get_mass_properties()
print(f"Volume: {volume:.2f} mm³")
print(f"Center of Gravity: {cog}")
print(f"Surface Area: {your_mesh.areas.sum():.2f} mm²")

# Number of triangles
print(f"Number of Triangles: {len(your_mesh.vectors)}")

Choosing Layer Height

Layer height is the most important parameter determining the trade-off between build quality and build time:

Layer Height Build Quality Build Time Typical Use
0.1 mm (ultra-fine) Very high (layer lines nearly invisible) Very long (×2-3) Figurines, medical models, end-use parts
0.2 mm (standard) Good (layer lines visible but acceptable) Standard General prototypes, functional parts
0.3 mm (coarse) Low (layer lines clearly visible) Short (×0.5) Early prototypes, internal structural parts
⚠️ Layer Height Constraints

Layer height should be set to 25-80% of the nozzle diameter. For example, with a 0.4 mm nozzle, the recommended layer-height range is 0.1-0.32 mm. Exceeding this range can cause insufficient extrusion or the nozzle dragging through the previous layer.

1.4.2 Shell and Infill Strategies

Shell Generation

The shell (perimeter) is the path that forms the outer boundary of each layer:

  • Shell count (perimeter count): typically 2-4 lines. Affects external quality and strength.
    • 1 line: very weak, high transparency, decorative use only
    • 2 lines: standard (good balance)
    • 3-4 lines: high strength, improved surface quality, better airtightness
  • Shell order: inside-out is common. Outside-in is used when surface quality is the priority.

Infill Patterns

Infill forms the internal structure, controlling strength and material usage:

Pattern Strength Print Speed Material Usage Characteristics
Grid Medium Fast Medium Simple, isotropic, a standard choice
Honeycomb High Slow Medium High strength, excellent strength-to-weight ratio, aerospace applications
Gyroid Very high Medium Medium 3D isotropic, curved geometry, a modern recommendation
Concentric Low Fast Low Prioritizes flexibility, follows the shell
Lines Low (anisotropic) Very fast Low High-speed printing, directional strength
💡 Infill Density Guidelines
  • 0-10%: decorative, non-load-bearing parts (prioritizing material savings)
  • 20%: standard prototypes (good balance)
  • 40-60%: functional parts requiring high strength
  • 100%: end-use parts requiring watertightness and maximum strength (build time ×3-5)

1.4.3 Support Structure Generation

Areas with overhang angles greater than 45 degrees require a support structure:

Types of Supports

  • Linear supports: vertical, column-shaped supports. Simple and easy to remove, but use more material.
  • Tree supports: tree-like branching supports. Reduce material usage by 30-50% and are easy to remove. The standard support type in Cura and PrusaSlicer.
  • Interface layers: thin bonding layers placed on top of supports. Easy to remove and improve surface quality. Typically 2-4 layers.

Key Support Parameters

Parameter Recommended Value Effect
Overhang Angle 45-60° Supports generated beyond this angle
Support Density 10-20% Higher density is more stable but harder to remove
Support Z Distance 0.2-0.3 mm Gap between support and part (affects ease of removal)
Interface Layers 2-4 layers Number of bonding layers (balances surface quality and removability)

1.4.6 Toolpath Optimization Strategies

An efficient toolpath improves build time, quality, and material usage:

  • Retraction: pulling the filament back during travel moves to prevent stringing.
    • Distance: 1-6 mm (4-6 mm for Bowden setups, 1-2 mm for direct-drive)
    • Speed: 25-45 mm/s
    • Excessive retraction can cause nozzle clogs
  • Z-hop: raising the nozzle during travel to avoid colliding with the printed part. Raises 0.2-0.5 mm. Slightly increases build time but improves surface quality.
  • Combing: restricts travel paths to the infill area, reducing travel marks on the surface. Useful when appearance matters.
  • Seam position: strategy for aligning the start/end point of each layer.
    • Random: random placement (less noticeable)
    • Aligned: placed in a straight line (easier to remove the seam in post-processing)
    • Sharpest Corner: placed at the sharpest corner (less noticeable)

# Rough estimate of build time (assuming 0.2mm layer height, 50mm/s speed)

import numpy as np
from stl import mesh

def check_normals(mesh_data):
    """Check the consistency of normal vectors in an STL mesh

    Args:
        mesh_data: a numpy-stl Mesh object

    Returns:
        tuple: (flipped_count, total_count, percentage)
    """
    # Verify normal direction using the right-hand rule
    flipped_count = 0
    total_count = len(mesh_data.vectors)

    for i, facet in enumerate(mesh_data.vectors):
        v0, v1, v2 = facet

        # Compute edge vectors
        edge1 = v1 - v0
        edge2 = v2 - v0

        # Compute normal via cross product (right-hand rule)
        calculated_normal = np.cross(edge1, edge2)

        # Normalize
        norm = np.linalg.norm(calculated_normal)
        if norm > 1e-10:  # confirm not a zero vector
            calculated_normal = calculated_normal / norm
        else:
            continue  # skip degenerate triangles

        # Compare with the normal stored in the file
        stored_normal = mesh_data.normals[i]
        stored_norm = np.linalg.norm(stored_normal)

        if stored_norm > 1e-10:
            stored_normal = stored_normal / stored_norm

        # Check directional agreement via dot product
        dot_product = np.dot(calculated_normal, stored_normal)

        # Negative dot product means it's flipped
        if dot_product < 0:
            flipped_count += 1

    percentage = (flipped_count / total_count) * 100 if total_count > 0 else 0

    return flipped_count, total_count, percentage

    """Generate a temperature profile

    Args:
        t (array): time array [min]
        T_target (float): hold temperature [°C]
        heating_rate (float): heating rate [°C/min]
        hold_time (float): hold time [min]
        cooling_rate (float): cooling rate [°C/min]

    Returns:
        array: temperature profile [°C]
    """
    T_room = 25  # room temperature
    T = np.zeros_like(t)

    # Heating duration
    t_heat = (T_target - T_room) / heating_rate

    # Cooling start time
    t_cool_start = t_heat + hold_time

    for i, time in enumerate(t):
        if time <= t_heat:
            # Heating phase
            T[i] = T_room + heating_rate * time
        elif time <= t_cool_start:
            # Hold phase
            T[i] = T_target
        else:
            # Cooling phase
            T[i] = T_target - cooling_rate * (time - t_cool_start)
            T[i] = max(T[i], T_room)  # never drops below room temperature

    return T

def simulate_reaction_progress(T, t, Ea, D0, r0):
    """Calculate reaction progress based on a temperature profile

    Args:
        T (array): temperature profile [°C]
        t (array): time array [min]
        Ea (float): activation energy [J/mol]
        D0 (float): pre-exponential factor [m²/s]
        r0 (float): particle radius [m]

    Returns:
        array: conversion fraction
    """
    R = 8.314
    C0 = 10000
    alpha = np.zeros_like(t)

    for i in range(1, len(t)):
        T_k = T[i] + 273.15
        D = D0 * np.exp(-Ea / (R * T_k))
        k = D * C0 / r0**2

        dt = (t[i] - t[i-1]) * 60  # min → s

        # Simple integration (reaction progress over a small time step)
        if alpha[i-1] < 0.99:
            dalpha = k * dt / (2 * (1 - (1-alpha[i-1])**(1/3)))
            alpha[i] = min(alpha[i-1] + dalpha, 1.0)
        else:
            alpha[i] = alpha[i-1]

    return alpha

1.7.1 Determining Rate Constants from Experimental Data

# ===================================
# Example 7: Fitting a Reaction Rate Curve
# ===================================

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

# Experimental data (time vs. conversion)
# Example: BaTiO3 synthesis @ 1200°C
time_exp = np.array([0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 15, 20])  # hours
conversion_exp = np.array([0, 0.15, 0.28, 0.38, 0.47, 0.60,
                          0.70, 0.78, 0.84, 0.90, 0.95])

# Jander model
def jander_model(t, k):
    """Calculate conversion using the Jander equation

    Args:
        t (array): time [hours]
        k (float): rate constant

    Returns:
        array: conversion fraction
    """
    # Solve [1 - (1-α)^(1/3)]² = kt for α
    kt = k * t
    alpha = 1 - (1 - np.sqrt(kt))**3
    alpha = np.clip(alpha, 0, 1)  # constrain to the 0-1 range
    return alpha

# Ginstling-Brounshtein equation (another diffusion model)
def gb_model(t, k):
    """Ginstling-Brounshtein equation

    Args:
        t (array): time
        k (float): rate constant

    Returns:
        array: conversion fraction
    """
    # 1 - 2α/3 - (1-α)^(2/3) = kt
    # Requires a numerical solution, but we use an approximation here
    kt = k * t
    alpha = 1 - (1 - kt/2)**(3/2)
    alpha = np.clip(alpha, 0, 1)
    return alpha

# Power law (empirical model)
def power_law_model(t, k, n):
    """Power-law model

    Args:
        t (array): time
        k (float): rate constant
        n (float): exponent

    Returns:
        array: conversion fraction
    """
    alpha = k * t**n
    alpha = np.clip(alpha, 0, 1)
    return alpha

# Fit each model
# Jander equation
popt_jander, _ = curve_fit(jander_model, time_exp, conversion_exp, p0=[0.01])
k_jander = popt_jander[0]

# Ginstling-Brounshtein equation
popt_gb, _ = curve_fit(gb_model, time_exp, conversion_exp, p0=[0.01])
k_gb = popt_gb[0]

# Power law
popt_power, _ = curve_fit(power_law_model, time_exp, conversion_exp, p0=[0.1, 0.5])
k_power, n_power = popt_power

# Generate predicted curves
t_fit = np.linspace(0, 20, 200)
alpha_jander = jander_model(t_fit, k_jander)
alpha_gb = gb_model(t_fit, k_gb)
alpha_power = power_law_model(t_fit, k_power, n_power)

# Compute residuals
residuals_jander = conversion_exp - jander_model(time_exp, k_jander)
residuals_gb = conversion_exp - gb_model(time_exp, k_gb)
residuals_power = conversion_exp - power_law_model(time_exp, k_power, n_power)

# Compute R²
def r_squared(y_true, y_pred):
    ss_res = np.sum((y_true - y_pred)**2)
    ss_tot = np.sum((y_true - np.mean(y_true))**2)
    return 1 - (ss_res / ss_tot)

1.8.1 Suppressing Grain Growth

In solid-state reactions, undesirable grain growth occurs with high-temperature, long-duration holds. Strategies to suppress it include:

  • Two-step sintering: a short hold at high temperature followed by a long hold at low temperature
  • Use of additives: adding trace amounts of grain-growth inhibitors (e.g., MgO, Al₂O₃)
  • Spark Plasma Sintering (SPS): rapid heating and short sintering times

1.8.2 Mechanochemical Activation of Reactions

Mechanochemical methods (high-energy ball milling) can also drive solid-state reactions near room temperature:

# ===================================
# Example 8: Grain Growth Simulation
# ===================================

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def grain_growth(t, T, D0, Ea, G0, n):
    """Time evolution of grain growth

    Burke-Turnbull equation: G^n - G0^n = k*t

    Args:
        t (array): time [hours]
        T (float): temperature [K]
        D0 (float): pre-exponential factor
        Ea (float): activation energy [J/mol]
        G0 (float): initial grain size [μm]
        n (float): grain growth exponent (typically 2-4)

    Returns:
        array: grain size [μm]
    """
    R = 8.314
    k = D0 * np.exp(-Ea / (R * T))
    G = (G0**n + k * t * 3600)**(1/n)  # hours → seconds
    return G

# Parameter settings
D0_grain = 1e8  # μm^n/s
Ea_grain = 400e3  # J/mol
G0 = 0.5  # μm
n = 3

# Effect of temperature

t_range = np.linspace(0, 12, 100)  # 0-12 hours

plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))

# Temperature dependence
plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)

Correct answer: c) The Binary format is typically 5-10× smaller in file size than the ASCII format

Explanation:

  • ASCII STL: a human-readable text format. Each triangle is described in 7 lines (facet, normal, 3 vertices, endfacet). Large file size (tens to hundreds of MB).

  • Modern 3D printing software supports both formats, though Binary is recommended
